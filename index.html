Motivation

How much of a score belongs to the model?

Unsupervised anomaly detection learns what healthy brain MRI looks like and flags whatever departs from it, so it needs no voxel-level annotation. New methods are usually compared through a single number on a public tumour dataset. That number is the end of a long chain of decisions taken after training: how each anomaly map is brought into the geometry of the reference annotation, how a threshold is chosen, how many false positives are tolerated, which metric is reported and how subjects are pooled. Most of them can be made in more than one defensible way, and few papers say which way they took.

MIRTO1 is an evaluation protocol built around that problem. It asks four things of any comparison between methods.

R

Registration-gated

No anomaly map is scored until it has been shown to sit in the same space as the reference annotation.

T

Threshold-transfer-aware

Thresholds are fixed on held-out validation data, and the protocol reports how well they carry over to the test set instead of assuming that they do.

I

Interval-based

Every difference between methods is reported with a paired, subject-level uncertainty interval, never as a bare point estimate.

M

Multiverse-tested

Results are reported across the whole space of defensible evaluation choices, so that readers can see which conclusions hold and which depend on one particular setting.

MIRTO is the protocol; MITRA2 is the study that tests it. MITRA re-evaluates contemporary unsupervised anomaly segmentation methods for brain MRI with matched training data, a common reference geometry and analysis rules fixed in advance, and asks a simple question: when a method wins, what exactly has won?

Where it started

Earlier presentations

MIRTO grew out of two earlier stages of the project, both presented by Negin.

December 2025 · Naples

BBCC 2025, Bioinformatics and Computational Biology Conference

Poster: Unsupervised brain MRI anomaly detection: revisiting the StRegA pipeline in the era of diffusion-based UAD

The starting point. StRegA simplifies its input by segmenting brain tissue before a compact context-encoding VAE models healthy anatomy, but its reconstructions are over-smooth and segmentation errors can hide small lesions. The poster set StRegA against newer diffusion, contrastive and distillation methods and proposed benchmarking them on IXI, MOOD and BraTS.

Poster Abstract

2026 · Girona

ESMRMB 2026, European Society for Magnetic Resonance in Medicine and Biology

Lightning talk (session LTD23) and poster (PG180): Benchmarking unsupervised anomaly detection in brain MRI: a systematic comparison across training regimes and preprocessing pipelines

The benchmark itself: five unsupervised anomaly detection methods, each trained in ten matched configurations on healthy scans and tested on BraTS 2020, with and without StRegA's tissue-segmentation preprocessing. Its conclusion, that the pipeline around a model can matter as much as the model itself, is where MIRTO begins.

Notes on the names

  1. MIRTO: Multiverse-tested, Interval-based, Registration-gated, Threshold-transfer-aware evaluatiOn. Mirto is the myrtle-berry liqueur of Sardinia. Our unsupervised anomaly detection work is named after Italian liqueurs: the first was StRegA, after Strega, the saffron-yellow herbal liqueur of Benevento, and MIRTO follows it. ↩
  2. MITRA: Multiverse Investigation of Training, Registration and Assessment choices. Mithra is an ancient Indo-Iranian deity of covenants, light, justice and the sun. In the Vedas he appears as Mitra, who with Varuna upholds friendship, contracts and cosmic order. In ancient Iran and in Zoroastrianism, Mithra became a major yazata (divine being) guarding truth, cattle, harvests and the bonds between people. The Roman mystery cult of Mithras, which emerged in the Roman Empire in the first century AD, took its inspiration from the Persian god. In Bengali, mitra (মিত্র) simply means 'friend'. ↩
