Abstract

In brief

Unsupervised anomaly detection (UAD) methods for brain MRI are ranked by a single score, yet that score rests on choices that are rarely reported: how each anomaly map is aligned with the reference, how and on which data the threshold is set, and which false-positive budget, metric, aggregation and lesion definition are used. We present MIRTO, an evaluation protocol that makes these choices explicit and measures their effect. It gates the geometry of every comparison with a registration check and label-free diagnostics of known power, sets thresholds on validation data alone and reports the false-positive volume actually realised on test, repeats each comparison over 15,552 defensible evaluation pipelines, and attaches paired subject-bootstrap intervals with multiplicity control. Applied to four UAD methods trained on the same healthy data and tested on 312 BraTS 2020 subjects, MIRTO showed that an axis-order mismatch between stored maps and the reference lowered a diffusion model's voxel AUROC from 0.873 to 0.583 whilst barely moving its slice-level AUROC. Within each metric, the method explained at least 0.95 of the variance in voxel AUROC and AUPRC and 0.77 in Dice, but only 0.14 in lesion sensitivity, where the lesion definition and hit criterion dominated. A Dice advantage that was significant at validation thresholds vanished at equal realised false-positive burden, and an exact identity attributes it to threshold transfer. A training-free change to REFLECT's latent aggregation raised Dice at equal burden by 0.052. Nine hypotheses were tested against explicit criteria; because the same cohort served to develop the protocol, all inference is exploratory.

How the project evolved

From a better model to a better question

MITRA began as an attempt to improve a model. It became a study of how models are judged. Three stages, each presented in public, mark the turn: a poster that proposed upgrading StRegA with newer methods, a benchmark that found the pipeline around a model could matter as much as the model, and MIRTO, which asks how much of any leaderboard the evaluation decides.

December 2025 · Naples · Poster

BBCC 2025: revisiting StRegA in the era of diffusion-based UAD

Bioinformatics and Computational Biology Conference. Poster: Unsupervised brain MRI anomaly detection: revisiting the StRegA pipeline in the era of diffusion-based UAD.

The question

Can StRegA's tissue-aware input still help when it is combined with the newer generative and contrastive models?

StRegA segments brain tissue with FSL FAST before a compact context-encoding VAE learns healthy anatomy. The simpler input lets a small model do the work, but its reconstructions tend to be over-smooth and can miss small lesions, and errors in the tissue segmentation can hide or misclassify disease. The abstract surveyed what had appeared since: scale-aware contrastive reverse distillation, conditional and guided diffusion models with sharper pseudo-healthy reconstructions, masked diffusion pre-training, and normative modelling of healthy variability. It proposed pairing StRegA's input simplification with these methods and validating the result on three complementary datasets: IXI for healthy anatomy, MOOD for synthetic anomalies and BraTS for real tumours.

Submitted abstract, in short

Unsupervised brain MRI anomaly detection with enhanced StRegA integrating diffusion models, multi-scale distillation and normative representation learning. The abstract set out StRegA's strengths (tissue-segmented input, a compact context-encoding VAE, less reliance on large architectures) and its limits (over-smooth reconstructions, dependence on the tissue segmentation). It argued that the newer methods perform well on benchmark datasets but that their behaviour on varied clinical groups was largely unknown, and proposed testing whether tissue-aware context improves robustness to scanner and anatomical variability when combined with diffusion and contrastive models. 30 September to 3 October 2026 · Girona · Lightning talk and poster

ESMRMB 2026: a benchmark across training regimes and preprocessing pipelines

European Society for Magnetic Resonance in Medicine and Biology. Lightning talk (session LTD23) and poster (PG180): Benchmarking unsupervised anomaly detection in brain MRI: a systematic comparison across training regimes and preprocessing pipelines.

The question

How much do contrast matching, multi-site pooling and tissue-segmentation preprocessing matter, compared with the design of the model?

Five methods (StRegA, SCRD4AD, MAD-AD, cDDPM and UCCD) were each trained on healthy scans from IXI and MOOD in ten matched configurations, covering single-site, mixed-field-strength and joint T1 and T2 regimes, and tested on BraTS. StRegA's FSL FAST preprocessing was also transferred to the four other methods on three representative configurations.

Contrast matching came first

Models trained on T2 and tested on BraTS T2 beat their T1 counterparts for every method. Mean slice AUROC, T1 against T2: StRegA 0.73 and 0.84, cDDPM 0.81 and 0.85, UCCD 0.81 and 0.88.

Conditioned diffusion led

UCCD reached slice AUROC 0.883 and large-tumour Dice 0.706 (IXI 1.5 T to BraTS T2); cDDPM reached 0.865 and 0.768 (IXI 3 T plus 1.5 T to BraTS T2).

More data did not always help

Pooling sites helped cDDPM but hurt MAD-AD (AUROC 0.794, 0.728, then 0.668 on T1) and SCRD4AD (0.721 to 0.590).

Preprocessing interacted with architecture

FAST input raised SCRD4AD's slice Dice from 0.348 to 0.754 and its small-tumour Dice from near zero to 0.265, the only non-trivial small-lesion result in the benchmark, whereas cDDPM fell from 0.531 to 0.184 and UCCD dropped to zero on its strongest configuration.

The abstract concluded that upstream choices shifted model rankings more than architecture did, and argued for reporting UAD performance across a matrix of conditions instead of a single configuration. That sentence is where MIRTO starts.

These numbers come from the benchmark's own evaluation pipeline. The preprint re-scores every map on one canonical reference with gated geometry and validation-only thresholds, so the two sets of numbers are not directly comparable.

MIRTO: auditing the evaluation itself

The question

How much of a UAD leaderboard is decided by the evaluation rather than by the model? And can a protocol make that dependence visible, catch the errors it hides, and still say something definite about the models?

The benchmark's conclusion became a hypothesis to test. The preprint holds the training data fixed (IXI T2 from Hammersmith Hospital at 3 T and Guy's Hospital at 1.5 T, the same for every method), scores four current UAD families and two model-free intensity baselines on 312 BraTS 2020 test subjects, and treats every evaluation decision as an explicit axis. Along the way it found that the mapping of anomaly maps into a common space, which most papers never mention, can quietly invalidate a comparison. Where possible, each component of the protocol was checked on cases in which the right answer is known: synthetic volumes, injected corruptions and random calibration splits.

The protocol

Four components, each tested where the answer is known

A UAD score is the end of a long chain of decisions taken after training. MIRTO takes the frozen anomaly maps of every method and passes them through four components before any claim is made; the final O, evaluatiOn, is the form in which every claim is reported. None of this retrains a model or runs new inference.

MMultiverse-tested10 axes · 15,552 pipelines IInterval-basedpaired bootstrap · Holm RRegistration-gatedone reference · 56 transforms TThreshold-transfer-awarevalidation-only τ · realised burden OevaluatiOnan indexed claim

The letters spell the name, not the order of execution. In practice the geometry gate runs first, thresholds are set next, the multiverse is evaluated over the gated maps, and an interval is attached to every contrast before it is reported.

M

Multiverse-tested

Ten axes of evaluation choice (reference, support, voxel volume, threshold protocol, budget, post-processing, lesion definition, hit criterion, aggregation and metric) give 155,520 universes per method, of which 15,552 are defensible. MIRTO reports how often each pair of methods swaps places, by how much each leads, and how much of the variance belongs to the method. The training set is added as an eleventh axis.

Caught The method explains at least 0.95 of the variance in voxel AUROC but only 0.14 in lesion sensitivity.

I

Interval-based

Every contrast carries a paired subject-bootstrap interval (104 resamples) with Holm adjustment inside declared families, and is stated for a given budget, aggregation and operating point. Nine hypotheses carry explicit decision criteria, and eight secondary hypotheses predict specific effects.

Caught Unpaired, unadjusted claims: each contrast is tied to the operating point at which it holds.

R

Registration-gated

Every subject has one reference on a 1 mm canonical grid, never resampled or cropped per method. Each method's maps are brought onto it by a repaired transform fixed before scoring, and a gate checks the result: centroid error within 3 mm, the applied transform the best of 56 candidates, and an overlap floor of 0.85. Five label-free diagnostics flag errors that slip through.

Caught An axis-order mismatch that left cDDPM's voxel AUROC at 0.583 instead of 0.873, whilst slice-level AUROC moved by only 0.112.

T

Threshold-transfer-aware

Thresholds are chosen on 24 validation subjects alone, for a nominal false-positive budget of 5, 10, 20 or 40 cm³. The protocol then reports the burden actually realised on test (mean, median, 95th percentile and the share of subjects above budget), the threshold-transfer gap, contrasts at equal realised burden, and a conformal per-scan guarantee.

Caught A UCCD advantage over cDDPM of +0.020 Dice at validation thresholds that became −0.001 at equal realised burden.

O

evaluatiOn: the indexed claim

What comes out is not a leaderboard but a set of indexed claims. Every claim names its geometry, threshold rule, budget, metric, aggregation and training data, and carries its paired interval, the false-positive burden actually realised and the share of defensible universes in which it holds.

Example REFLECT has the highest voxel AUROC (0.932 against 0.865 to 0.873 for the diffusion models) in every universe and with every training set.

Exact identities accompany the metrics. They split a contrast at validation thresholds into a map term and a transfer term, split a post-processing gain at equal burden into a fixed-threshold effect and a threshold relaxation, and decompose pooled Dice and pooled AUROC so that disagreements between subject-level and pooled rankings can be explained rather than merely observed. A known result on split-conformal calibration states, from the calibration size alone, how often a small calibration set will miss its target.

Findings

What the protocol showed

All four methods (REFLECT, UCCD, cDDPM and AnomalyDINO) were trained on the same healthy data and scored on the same 312 test subjects. Intervals are 95% paired subject-bootstrap intervals.

01 · Registration

A geometric error that standard metrics miss

Each method stores its maps in its own axis order, crop and resolution, so any comparison has to undo those steps. Reading cDDPM's stored maps in the wrong axis order, and ignoring their crop, gave a voxel AUROC of 0.583; the correct mapping gave 0.873. Slice-level AUROC, a widely reported detection score, barely noticed, because a slice that contains tumour still contains it after any in-plane permutation.

Under the wrong mapping, 24% of cDDPM test subjects were anti-located (per-subject voxel AUROC below 0.5). Two further errors of the same kind occurred with REFLECT. Each produced a plausible table. On correct pipelines the gate passed 1,342 of 1,344 method and subject pairs, and two label-free diagnostics detected every injected in-plane flip and transpose with power 1.000 at a false-alarm rate of 0.05, already with 30 subjects.

02 · Multiverse

Some rankings belong to the method, others to the evaluation

Voxel AUROC gave one order in every universe: REFLECT, then cDDPM, UCCD and AnomalyDINO. Dice did not. The method still explained 0.77 of its variance, but most of that is AnomalyDINO's deficit; amongst the other three, every pair swapped places in 36% to 49% of Dice universes, Dice produced six different orders, and each of REFLECT, UCCD and cDDPM ranked first in between 26% and 39% of them. Lesion sensitivity was governed by the evaluation, with the lesion definition and the hit criterion carrying most of the variance.

A flip share near one half is also what two indistinguishable methods produce, so MIRTO reads every flip share alongside the size of the difference. REFLECT and UCCD were within 0.02 Dice of each other in only 26.9% of Dice universes: each leads by several points in a large share of defensible evaluations, and the evaluation decides which.

03 · Thresholds

A nominal budget is not an operating point

A threshold chosen on validation data to allow 20 cm³ of false positives does not deliver 20 cm³ on test. The mean realised burden ranged from 12.9 to 18.9 cm³, the median from 3.0 to 10.1, the 95th percentile from 37 to 64, and between 15.7% and 29.8% of subjects exceeded the budget. The mean describes no typical subject; a minority with very large false-positive volumes pulls it up.

The consequence for a ranking is direct. At validation thresholds UCCD beat cDDPM at every budget. Held to the same realised burden, the difference vanished at every budget. An exact identity accounts for the change: the shift between the two contrasts equals cDDPM's threshold-transfer gap minus UCCD's, 0.023 − 0.002 = 0.021, against an observed 0.021. The advantage belonged to how UCCD's threshold transferred, not to its map.

The same accounting applies to post-processing. Hysteresis with persistence pruning raised UCCD's Dice at equal burden by 0.085 at 20 cm³, but 0.078 of that came from the burden it freed for the plain threshold, and only 0.006 (interval including zero) from the step itself. For REFLECT the step alone lowered Dice. Conformal risk control at α = 0.10 kept the share of scans above budget at 0.032 to 0.096, against 0.157 to 0.298 with validation thresholds, at a cost of 0.053 to 0.116 Dice; with 24 calibration subjects the guarantee holds on average but fails for roughly three calibration sets in ten, as the exact Beta law of split-conformal calibration predicts.

04 · Detection

Large lesions found, small ones missed, and a sensitivity that depends on its denominator

At validation thresholds the three leading models found 91.5% to 99.7% of large lesions and 3.6% to 7.3% of small ones: at most 12 of 165. They missed largely the same lesions, and lesion volume separated hits from misses almost completely. Most misses still carried evidence above the subject's healthy-brain median that the threshold did not select.

The lesion definition alone rescaled pooled sensitivity about elevenfold. REFLECT hit 287 lesions under both definitions: 287 of 472 with a 50 mm³ floor, 287 of 5,473 without. A lesion-level metric is uninterpretable without its connectivity and floor.

Slice-level AUROC needs similar care. A scorer that knows only how much brain a slice contains reached 0.884, within 0.025 of every leading model. Replacing each subject's slice scores with those of the 50 subjects of most similar brain area lowered every model's AROC, by 0.124 to 0.271, so every model did carry subject-specific information.

05 · Training data

Change the training set for everyone, and a significant contrast reverses

With the evaluation held fixed, REFLECT minus UCCD Dice was −0.042 [−0.060, −0.025] when all four methods were trained on IXI-HH+Guys and +0.100 [0.079, 0.121] when all were trained on MOOD+IXI. The MOOD+IXI comparison adds T1 data for every method, so the IXI-HH+Guys comparison is the clean one. Choosing a training set per method from the 24 validation subjects was itself unstable: the primary rule chose IXI-HH+Guys for REFLECT in only 36.8% of 104 bootstrap resamples.

06 · A method-level gain

A training-free change to REFLECT

REFLECT's four latent channels need not agree on where an anomaly lies, and its published map averages them. Aggregating the channels by their disagreement, here the median absolute deviation, raised Dice at equal burden by +0.052 [0.045, 0.058] for REFLECT trained on IXI-HH+Guys and by +0.038 [0.033, 0.043] for an independently trained REFLECT on MOOD+IXI. Every latent aggregation improved on the published map in both models, and scoring with the latent stream alone hurt. The specific winner did not replicate across the two models, so the claim is for the family of latent aggregations, not for one rule.

Hypotheses

Nine hypotheses, nine decision criteria

Each hypothesis was stated with an explicit criterion before its verdict was read. Four were supported, four were not, and one could be tested only in part. Seven of the eight secondary hypotheses held. Because the 312 test subjects also served to develop the protocol, every test is exploratory.

For authors of UAD methods

What we suggest reporting

  1. Score every method against one reference on one grid, and report a registration check.
  2. Choose thresholds on data that are not the test set, and report the realised false-positive burden as a distribution: median and 95th percentile, not only the mean.
  3. Report subject-mean, median and pooled Dice side by side. When two methods swap places across evaluation choices, report by how much each leads.
  4. State the lesion definition in full: connectivity, floor and hit criterion.
  5. Do not report marginal slice AUROC without an anatomy control.
  6. Report paired intervals with a declared multiplicity correction.
  7. Train competing methods on the same healthy data, or say plainly that they were not.

The checklist released with the code expands this list.

Limitations, as stated in the paper

The evidence comes from one cohort and one contrast. BraTS 2020 whole-tumour labels are large, conspicuous lesions, so the small-lesion results are a floor, not a characterisation. The 312 test subjects served both to develop the protocol and the choice of training set and to test the hypotheses, so all inference is exploratory. In particular, the common training set was fixed after all twenty combinations of method and training set had been scored on the test cohort; the paper states the rationale for the choice without reference to any score and shows that each evaluation finding holds with every training set. One trained model exists per method and training set, so training-seed variance is unknown. A common training set means the same source data, not the same optimisation budget, and IXI-HH+Guys is not the best training set for every method. The diagnostics were validated on two pipelines. Validation-threshold intervals do not include the uncertainty of fitting a threshold on 24 subjects. The next step is a pre-registered confirmatory evaluation on an untouched cohort.
